    <p class="mf-standfirst">A journal for work that has stopped — and that cannot be restarted without becoming something else. For the researchers who have such work, and the readers who want to see what research looks like before it is asked to settle.</p>

      <section class="mf-section" id="mf-01">
        <div class="contrib-section-head">
            <div class="kicker">What we publish.</div>
            <h2>Before it is<br>finished.</h2>
        </div>
        <p>
          <em>The Journal of Incomplete Studies</em> publishes research that has stopped short of where publication usually expects it to arrive. Work in progress. Work interrupted or abandoned. Evidence that could not carry the claim it was gathered for. A question that outlived its method. An impasse that was reached and left in place.
        </p>
        <p>
          Think of a fieldwork archive that closed when access did. A corpus that proved too thin for its hypothesis. A translation abandoned halfway because of what the untranslatable passage revealed. A model that failed in a way that taught more than success would have. Each of these ends without becoming a finished study, and each leaves behind something the finished form would not have carried.
        </p>
        <p>
          These materials can appear as a preface, a footnote, or a limitations paragraph in the study that did arrive. They rarely appear as work in their own right.
        </p>
        <p class="mf-pull">
          Incomplete, here, does not mean unfinished in the sense of "not yet done." It means stopped: a study that has reached a state it cannot leave without becoming a different study.
        </p>
        <p>
          The distinction matters. "Not yet done" implies a trajectory. "Stopped" implies a state. The journal publishes work in the second sense — work whose stopping is itself the finding.
        </p>
        <div class="about-dual">
          <div class="dual-card">
            <div class="dual-col">
              <span class="dual-label">Sense 1</span>

            </div>
            <div class="dual-divider">

            </div>
            <div class="dual-col">
              <span class="dual-label">Sense 2</span>
              <span class="dual-title">A journal studying</span>
              <span class="dual-title-sub">incompleteness itself</span>
            </div>
          </div>
        </div>
        <p>
          Incompletion is not a defect to be corrected. It is a condition with its own weight — and that weight changes what a study can claim, and what a form can carry. The usual form of scholarly publication is not enough, because it is built for studies that have arrived.
        </p>
        <p>
          Reading incomplete work requires a different attention. The question is not <em>what does this prove?</em> but <em>what does this show?</em> Not <em>what is the argument?</em> but <em>where did the argument stop, and why?</em> The reader is not a judge. The reader is a witness.
        </p>
      </section>

      <section class="mf-section" id="mf-02">
        <div class="contrib-section-head">
          <div class="kicker">What the word carries</div>
          <h2>Complete according<br>to what?</h2>
        </div>

        <p>
          In ordinary usage, <em>incomplete</em> describes something that is not whole, not finished, or does not contain all the parts or information <em>expected of it</em>. That last phrase matters. To call something incomplete is already to measure it against an expectation of what it should contain, how it should conclude, or what form it should take. A study is incomplete because something is missing from the form it is expected to become.
        </p>

        <p>
          But there is more than one way for something to be incomplete. It may simply be <em>not yet finished</em>: still moving toward a conclusion, a publication, a result. It may be incomplete because it <em>cannot be completed</em>: the evidence is unavailable, the project is interrupted, the method fails, the circumstances change. It may be incomplete because <em>completion is not appropriate to it</em>: forcing the work into a settled argument or conventional article would misrepresent what the research has found. And it may be complete in one sense while remaining incomplete in another: the research process may be over, the evidence assembled, the work published, and yet the question may remain unresolved.
        </p>

        <p class="mf-pull">
          Not everything incomplete is waiting to become complete.
        </p>

        <p>
          These distinctions matter because they separate incompletion from simple lateness. Sometimes what is missing is a missing piece. Sometimes it is a limit. Sometimes it is an unanswered question. Sometimes the expectation of completion belongs more to the form we use to recognize research than to the research itself.
        </p>

        <p>
          This is where <em>incomplete</em> becomes more than a description of unfinished work. An incomplete study can be one that has reached a state it cannot leave without becoming a different study. More can happen to it, but what happens next may no longer be completion in any simple sense. It may be another question, another method, another argument, another work. The study's incompletion is therefore not merely what it lacks. It is part of the condition in which it exists.
        </p>

        <p class="mf-pull">
          The question is not simply whether a study is complete. It is: complete according to what expectation?
        </p>

        <p>
          What does completion require the work to become? And what might be lost when research is made to satisfy that expectation?
        </p>
      </section>

      <section class="mf-section" id="mf-025">
        <div class="contrib-section-head">
            <div class="kicker">What completion resolves.</div>
            <h2>What it leaves out.</h2>
        </div>
        <p>
          The conventional research article is a powerful form. It gives readers a method, evidence, an argument and a claim to evaluate. It is also a form that must resolve in order to exist.
        </p>
        <p>
          Whatever was uncertain earlier has been resolved, contained or excluded. The method that failed is not the method the article reports. The question that could not be answered is not the question it asks. The evidence that contradicted the conclusion is not the evidence it carries.
        </p>
        <p class="mf-pull">
          The finished study tells us what the research resolved. Incomplete work can also tell us what it could not resolve, and what resolution left behind.
        </p>
        <p>
          This is what makes the finished article useful, and what makes it partial. It is simply a form designed to hold only what has been settled. Some results are not failures awaiting settlement; they are results that form cannot hold.
        </p>
        <p>
          The journal opens that form. It wants to meet research while its uncertainties are still visible: failed procedures, abandoned hypotheses, partial datasets. It wants the unresolved interpretations, the changed questions, the contradictory observations, the records of decisions not to continue.
        </p>
      </section>

      <section class="mf-section" id="mf-03">
        <div class="contrib-section-head">
          <div class="kicker">What incompletion is not</div>
          <h2>What this is not.</h2>
        </div>
        <p>
          A journal of incomplete studies invites a set of assumptions. Here are the ones we do not hold.
        </p>
        <p>
          This is not a journal of failure. Failure is one state among many, and it is not the only one worth publishing. This is not a journal of process. Process implies a trajectory; the work here has stopped. This is not a journal of fragments. A fragment can be as closed as an essay. This is not a journal of the unfinished in general. It is a journal of work that has stopped — and whose stopping is itself the finding.
        </p>
        <p>
          Nor is this a journal of consolation. It does not exist to make researchers feel better about work that did not complete. It exists because that work carries something the finished form cannot — and because that something deserves readers.
        </p>
        <p>
          And it is not a journal of excuses. An unfinished draft can pass itself off as a considered position, and the vocabulary of provisionality can excuse almost anything. The journal's answer is not to lower its standard but to change what the standard measures. Stopping is not a licence to skip the writing.
        </p>
        <p>
          The distinction matters because each misreading would change what the journal asks of its contributors. A journal of failure would ask for post-mortems. A journal of process would ask for timelines. A journal of fragments would ask for pieces. Each of these is easier to write than what this journal asks for. This journal asks for something else: an account of its stopping — where the work stands, what stopped it, and what can and cannot be built on it. That is a harder thing to write — and it is the only thing that would justify publishing work in this state.
        </p>
      </section>

      <section class="mf-section" id="mf-04">
        <div class="contrib-section-head">
          <div class="kicker">What incompletion produces</div>
          <h2>What the work found instead.</h2>
        </div>
        <p>
          If the finished form cannot carry everything research produces, then what it cannot carry deserves to be read on its own terms. The journal makes two claims, matching the two senses of its title. Incomplete work is worth publishing. And incompletion is worth studying — not because it yields findings that completion would not, but because incompletion is itself a kind of finding.
        </p>
        <p class="mf-pull">
          Incomplete work is not the absence of a finding. It is a finding of a different kind.
        </p>
        <p>
          A failed experiment is not a method that has not yet succeeded. It is a method that has reached its limit, and the limit is what the research found. Missing evidence is not evidence that has not yet been gathered. It is a statement about the conditions under which evidence can exist.
        </p>
        <p>
          An abandoned field site is not a site that was left. It is a record of the arrangements that made the research possible — and of what would have to change for it to be possible again. An argument that will not settle is not an argument that has not yet been made. It is a demonstration that the object resists a single account. A study that never began is not a study that has not yet started. It is a record of the conditions that made it impossible — and of what would have to change for it to be possible at all.
        </p>
        <p>
          Consider the corpus that proved too thin for its hypothesis. The conventional article cannot carry it: there is no result to report, no claim to defend. But the thinness itself is a finding — about the conditions under which the hypothesis could be tested, about what would have been required, about the gap between the question and the evidence available to answer it.
        </p>
        <p>
          The record has widened in recent years, and it has not widened enough. Preprints, registered reports, and null-result journals each admit work the conventional article would exclude — and each still assumes a finished study underneath. What none of them admits is work whose state is the finding. That is the gap this journal occupies.
        </p>
        <p>
          What follows from this is not a relaxation of standards but a change in what the standard measures. The question is no longer whether the work arrived, but whether its account of stopping can be read by a stranger — whether it says, plainly, where the work stands and what stopped it. That question belongs to the writing, and the rest of this manifesto belongs to it too.
        </p>
      </section>

      <section class="mf-section" id="mf-05">
        <div class="contrib-section-head">
            <div class="kicker">What incompletion is</div>
            <h2>States, not stages.</h2>
        </div>
        <p>
          Research does not pass through incompletion on its way to completion. It can stop in a state and stay there. An argument can remain unsettled. Evidence can remain insufficient. A question can outlast the project that produced it.
        </p>
        <p>
          The states are not stages. They are conditions a study can be in — and remain in:
        </p>
        <p class="mf-states-line">
          <strong>In progress. Provisional. Uncertain. Interrupted. Abandoned. Inaccessible. Partial. Failed. Inconclusive. Unresolved.</strong>
        </p>
        <p>
          Each is a place a study can stop and remain. When a study stops, what it leaves is not a lesser version of a finished study. It is a study in a different state, carrying things the finished form cannot. The states can appear in different parts of a study. Each kind of incompletion carries something different:
        </p>
        <div class="mf-types">

          <div class="mf-type">
            <span class="mf-type-label">Conceptual</span>
            <span class="mf-type-desc">An argument has not settled, or competing interpretations remain in play.</span>
          </div>

          <div class="mf-type">
            <span class="mf-type-label">Empirical</span>
            <span class="mf-type-desc">Evidence is partial, contradictory, inaccessible, or insufficient for the claim it was gathered for.</span>
          </div>

          <div class="mf-type">
            <span class="mf-type-label">Methodological</span>
            <span class="mf-type-desc">A method has failed, produced unexpected results, or revealed a problem that changes the research.</span>
          </div>

          <div class="mf-type">
            <span class="mf-type-label">Historical</span>
            <span class="mf-type-desc">A record was lost, destroyed, or never existed, and the research cannot proceed without it.</span>
          </div>

          <div class="mf-type">
            <span class="mf-type-label">Formal</span>
            <span class="mf-type-desc">The work does not yet have a stable shape, or deliberately refuses one.</span>
          </div>

          <div class="mf-type">
            <span class="mf-type-label">Question-based</span>
            <span class="mf-type-desc">The research clarifies a problem without resolving it, leaving the question itself as the result.</span>
          </div>

        </div>
        <p>
          These are not categories to sort work into. They are descriptions of what stopped it — and what it left behind. A study can be in more than one state at once; most are.
        </p>
        <p>
          Some studies here may continue elsewhere. Some may become conventional publications. Others may stay unresolved for good. The journal asks for no prediction — only that the state of the work be described clearly enough to be read.
        </p>
        <p>
          There is no threshold. A study does not have to be abandoned, or failed, or partial in any particular way. It has to have stopped — and its stopping has to be describable. If you can say where the work stands and what stopped it, it belongs here.
        </p>
      </section>

      <section class="mf-section" id="mf-06">
        <div class="contrib-section-head">
            <div class="kicker">What the writing takes</div>
            <h2>The shape of a state.</h2>
        </div>
        <p>
          The journal's standard is not whether the work is finished, but whether its form fits its state. This is a hard standard, and it puts the measure in the writing.
        </p>
        <p class="mf-pull">
          Not whether the work is finished, but whether its form fits its state.
        </p>
        <p>
          That puts the measure in the writing, and scholars are trained to write against it. Scholarly prose closes in at least three ways at once: rhetorically, by ending on a claim; epistemically, by presenting the question as settled; socially, by reaching a point where the work can be handed off and cited. The journal has no quarrel with these in themselves. Its quarrel is with their being applied to work that has not earned them. In an article that closes, the doubts and qualifications that once surrounded each statement drop away as it hardens into a result. Writing that does not close keeps them in place, attached to what they qualify.
        </p>
        <p>
          This is not the same as confession. A candid account of how the research went is still told from the end, where every difficulty has a place and a purpose. It resolves the work retroactively and turns a state into a stage.
        </p>
        <p>
          The writing asked for here refuses that arrangement. The abandoned hypothesis stays abandoned. The changed question stays visibly changed. The failed method is not recast as a step toward the method that worked. No account is a transcript of a state — but an account can decline the one story most research writing tells without noticing: that everything that happened was heading toward what was found.
        </p>
        <p>
          An atmosphere of unfinishedness is not information. Nor is this a matter of style. A fragment can be as closed as an essay, and an ending that gestures at the unfinished is still an ending. The journal asks for something plainer and harder: that the writing say, in terms a stranger can check, where the work stands, what stopped it, and what a reader can and cannot build on.
        </p>
        <p>
          What shape this takes is not settled in advance. The article closes by design and the research note by abbreviation. Even the essay, the form most at home with doubt, tends to end by gathering itself up. Writing that holds a state has no ready-made form, and takes its shape from the state: from a thinking that has not stopped, an argument that has not settled, a question that has outlasted the attempt to answer it.
        </p>
        <p>
          The measure is fit, not display. A form fits when it tells the reader something true about where the work stands, and fails when it only resembles unfinishedness. There is no template. There is only the refusal of an ending the subject has not reached.
        </p>
        <p class="mf-final-question">
          This manifesto is itself an incomplete study. It describes a state the journal is in, not a position it has settled. It will be revised as we learn what we publish. Its current form is the shape of a question we have not finished asking.
        </p>
      </section>
