      <!-- Writing in public — the practice -->
      <section class="contrib-section" id="c-public">
        <div class="contrib-section-head">
          <div class="kicker">Writing in public</div>
          <h2>Before it is<br>finished.</h2>
        </div>

        <div class="contrib-prose">
          <p>
            Most research becomes public only once it is over. The argument is settled, the evidence is assembled, the method is justified, the conclusion is drawn. What the reader sees is the result — not the process that produced it, and not the uncertainty that accompanied it along the way.
          </p>
          <p>
            This journal asks contributors to do something different: to publish while the research is still moving. Not because the work is unfinished, but because the unfinished part is where the thinking actually happens — and because that thinking is worth sharing before it hardens into a claim.
          </p>
          <p class="contrib-pull">
            Writing in public means making the process visible: what was tried, what failed, what changed, and what remains unresolved.
          </p>
          <p>
            This is not the same as thinking out loud. A published installment is still a considered act — written for a reader, shaped by an editor, held to the same standards of precision as any scholarly work. What changes is not the rigor, but the point at which the work becomes visible.
          </p>
          <p>
            It also changes what the work can do. An installment written mid-inquiry can invite response, correction, and collaboration in a way that a finished article cannot. It can be wrong in public, and be corrected in public. It can change its mind across a season, and let the change be part of the record rather than a revision hidden from view.
          </p>
          <p>
            Writing in public is not for everyone, and it is not for every project. But for research that is genuinely still in motion — that has not yet settled into its final shape — it can be the most honest form the work can take.
          </p>
        </div>
      </section>

      <section class="contrib-section" id="c-what">
        <div class="contrib-section-head">
          <div class="kicker">What a contribution is</div>
          <h2>An installment,<br>not a draft.</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="contrib-prose">
          <p>
            In practice, an installment might begin with a question that has shifted, present the evidence that shifted it, acknowledge what remains unclear, and end by naming the next thing the researcher intends to investigate — or the reason they cannot.
          </p>
          <p>
            An installment runs <strong>2,000–6,000 words</strong>. A season typically involves <strong>three installments across four months</strong>, though the cadence can flex depending on the project. Each is published as it is written, not held until the season closes.
          </p>
          <p>
            It is a considered account of where a piece of research has reached: written for a reader, published while the inquiry is still in motion, and shaped by the state the work is actually in rather than the state it is supposed to be in.
          </p>
          <p class="contrib-pull">
            An installment is neither a final account nor a draft made public before it is ready. It is a considered scholarly act: an account of where the research has reached, what it can currently claim, and what remains uncertain.
          </p>
          <p>
            You are not being asked to publish your notes. You are being asked to write — to give the current state of the work a form that another person can read, respond to, and think with. An installment is not a placeholder for the real thing. It is the real thing, in the form it can currently take.
          </p>
        </div>
      </section>

      <!-- What's expected — what standard is expected? -->
      <section class="contrib-section" id="c-standards">
        <div class="contrib-section-head">
          <div class="kicker">What's expected</div>
          <h2>What rigor means<br>when nothing is settled.</h2>
        </div>

        <div class="contrib-prose">
          <p>
            In most scholarly contexts, rigor is measured against completion. Peer review assumes the argument has settled. Revision assumes the evidence has been assembled. Publication assumes the work has reached a point where it can be judged as a whole. Standards are applied to finished things.
          </p>
          <p>
            This journal asks for rigor without that assumption. The work is not finished, and it is not being judged as if it were. So the standards have to be built differently — not lowered, but relocated. Instead of asking whether the conclusions hold, we ask whether the claims are precise. Instead of asking whether the argument is complete, we ask whether its current limits are honestly described.
          </p>
          <p>
            An installment should be exact about its claims, attentive to its evidence, and clear about what has and has not been established. A failed method can remain a failure. A contradiction does not need to be smoothed away simply because it is difficult to explain. An open question does not need to be closed for the sake of appearance. The work can be rigorous without being made to appear settled. Polish is welcome; pretending is not.
          </p>
        </div>

        <div class="standards-grid">
          <div class="standard">
            <div class="standard-head">
              <i class="std-mark yes"></i>
              <b>What we ask for</b>
            </div>
            <ul class="standard-list">
              <li>Precision about claims, evidence, and method</li>
              <li>Clarity about what has and has not been established</li>
              <li>Willingness to keep uncertainty visible</li>
              <li>A commitment to the question across the season</li>
            </ul>
          </div>

          <div class="standard">
            <div class="standard-head">
              <i class="std-mark no"></i>
              <b>What you will not be asked to pretend</b>
            </div>
            <ul class="standard-list">
              <li>That the argument has settled</li>
              <li>That the method succeeded</li>
              <li>That the arc is complete</li>
              <li>That uncertainty has been resolved</li>
            </ul>
          </div>
        </div>

        <div class="contrib-prose contrib-prose-after">
          <p>
            The standard is not completion. It is honesty about what the work can currently support.
          </p>
        </div>
      </section>

        <!-- What a season asks — what's the commitment? -->
      <section class="contrib-section" id="c-commitment">
        <div class="contrib-section-head">
          <div class="kicker">What a season asks</div>
          <h2>A commitment<br>with an end.</h2>
        </div>

        <div class="contrib-prose">
          <p>
            A project enters the journal through an initial installment and continues across the season. A season typically involves <strong>three installments across four months</strong>, though the cadence can flex depending on the project. Installments run 2,000–6,000 words each.
          </p>
          <p>
            The season is a bounded commitment. It has a beginning, a cadence, and an end — and the end is not a completion requirement. The season provides the occasion for stopping; it does not require a reason beyond the work's own condition. When the season closes, you are free to continue, to pause, or to stop.
          </p>
          <p class="contrib-pull">
            The season is not a demand for a finished project. It is a way out of one — a form that lets the work stop without treating the stop as a failure.
          </p>
          <p>
            This matters because incompletion is often framed as something missing. A missing conclusion, a missing arc, a missing final account. But a season reframes it: the research does not have to end in completion to end well. It can end because the question has been pursued as far as it can currently go, because the evidence has run out, because the contributor has reached a natural resting point, or because continuing would no longer be honest. None of these are failures. They are forms of <em>incompletion</em> that the journal is built to hold.
          </p>
          <p>
            The research can also change between appearances. New evidence may alter an argument. A question may shift. A method may be reconsidered. An unexpected result may redirect the work. The next installment does not have to confirm the last.
          </p>
          <p>
            This is why the journal is structured across time. The installments are not chapters waiting to be assembled into a finished paper. Each marks a different point in the movement of the research — and the season, as a whole, marks a stretch of that movement with a clear beginning and a clear end.
          </p>
          <p>
            Earlier installments remain in the archive. Later versions do not replace them. A contributor may revise an argument, contradict an earlier position, extend the project, change direction, or stop altogether. What follows can change the meaning of what came before, but it does not erase the point from which the research proceeded.
          </p>
          <p>
            A contribution therefore becomes part of a record: what was thought, what was attempted, what changed, and what remained unresolved. The season gives that record a shape — a defined stretch of inquiry with a beginning, a cadence, and an end — but the record itself remains, open to whatever follows.
          </p>
        </div>
      </section>

      <!-- How to apply — how do I start? -->
      <section class="contrib-section" id="c-apply">
        <div class="contrib-section-head">
          <div class="kicker">How to apply</div>
          <h2>How a project<br>enters the journal.</h2>
        </div>

        <div class="contrib-prose">
          <p>
            A project enters the journal through a proposal. This is not a draft of the work — it is a short account of it: 500 words describing where the research has reached, what question it is pursuing, and what state it is currently in. No writing sample is required at this stage. If your project is selected, the initial installment is written during the season, with editorial support.
          </p>
          <p>
            Before you write a proposal, it is worth spending time with the journal. Read <a href="{{ site.baseurl }}/manifesto/">the manifesto</a>, which explains why it exists. Read <a href="{{ site.baseurl }}/projects/">a project or two</a>, which show what an installment looks like in practice. Together they will tell you more about whether your work belongs here than any set of guidelines could.
          </p>
          <p class="contrib-pull">
            Proposals are read by the editorial team — every one by a person, not a committee. Responses are written, personal, and usually arrive within four weeks. We do not use anonymous peer review.
          </p>
          <p>
            Every proposal receives a response, whether or not the project is selected. If we say no, we will tell you why, and you are welcome to submit again in a future season.
          </p>
          <p>
            Proposals are accepted twice a year, in September and March. Each season's cohort is announced at its start, and publication runs across the following three to four months, with each contributor working alongside an editor to shape the cadence, the through-line of the inquiry, and the form each installment might take.
          </p>
          <p>
            When you are ready, the submission form is the only thing you need. There is no fee, no formatting requirement, and no need to wait for a call for papers. If you are unsure whether your project fits — or whether it is too early, too late, or too strange — write to us and ask.
          </p>
        </div>

      <section class="contrib-section" id="c-faq">
        <div class="contrib-section-head">
          <div class="kicker">Common questions</div>
          <h2>Before writing<br>to ask.</h2>
        </div>

        <div class="about-faq">

          <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
              <span>Do I need an academic affiliation?</span>
              <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
              <p>No. We publish independent researchers, artists, practitioners, and scholars alike.</p>
            </div>
          </details>

          <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
              <span>Isn't writing in public just blogging?</span>
              <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
              <p>No. Blogging tends to be informal, unedited, and written for immediacy. An installment is a considered scholarly act: written for a reader, shaped by an editor, and held to the same standards of precision as any academic work. What changes is the point at which the work becomes visible — not the rigor with which it is made.</p>
            </div>
          </details>

          <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
              <span>Can I submit a project that is already partly published elsewhere?</span>
              <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
              <p>Yes, if you hold the rights and can note where the earlier material appeared.</p>
            </div>
          </details>

          <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
              <span>Can I submit more than one project?</span>
              <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
              <p>One project per season, per contributor.</p>
            </div>
          </details>

          <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
              <span>What if I change direction or stop?</span>
              <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
              <p>The journal is built for this. Later installments may revise, contradict, or replace earlier ones. A project may be paused and resumed, or stopped without being completed. Earlier installments remain in the archive regardless of what follows.</p>
            </div>
          </details>

          <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
              <span>How are submissions reviewed?</span>
              <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
              <p>Every submission is read by a person, not a committee. Responses are written, personal, and usually arrive within four weeks. We do not use anonymous peer review.</p>
            </div>
          </details>

          <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
              <span>Is there a submission fee?</span>
              <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
              <p>No. There are no submission fees, processing charges, or paywalls. The journal is open access and always free to read.</p>
            </div>
          </details>

          <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
              <span>What rights do I retain?</span>
              <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
              <p>Contributors retain copyright. Work is published under a CC BY-NC 4.0 license, which allows others to share and adapt the work for non-commercial purposes with attribution.</p>
            </div>
          </details>

          <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
              <span>What format should I submit in?</span>
              <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
              <p>We accept .docx, .pdf, and plain text. There is no required style guide at the proposal stage; if your project is selected, we will discuss formatting conventions together.</p>
            </div>
          </details>

          <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
              <span>Can I submit in a language other than English?</span>
              <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
              <p>The journal publishes in English. If you are working in another language and would like to propose a translation or bilingual contribution, write to us — we are open to conversations about this.</p>
            </div>
          </details>
