        <!-- Why the rule exists -->
        <section class="about-section" id="why">
        <div class="about-section-head">
            <div class="kicker">Why the rule</div>
            <h2>Research is a process,<br>not a product.</h2>
        </div>
        <div class="about-prose">
            <p>
            Most journals publish finished work. They treat incompleteness as a stage to be passed through: the draft becomes the paper, the paper becomes the citation, and the earlier stages fall away. The reader who arrives late sees only the finished thing, and the history of how it became that thing is lost.
            </p>
            <p>
            This journal does not do that. It publishes the process itself — as installments, not articles. A project enters as a question and continues through every state it passes through: revised, contradicted, paused, resumed, abandoned. Each of these states remains visible. Nothing is hidden by progress.
            </p>
            <p>
            What this produces is not a record of <em>results</em>. It is a record of <em>thinking</em> — including the thinking that went nowhere, and the thinking that changed its mind.
            </p>
        </div>
        </section>

        <!-- What incompletion means -->
        <section class="about-section" id="incompletion">
            <div class="about-section-head">
                <div class="kicker">What incompletion means</div>
                <h2>Not a stage —<br>a condition.</h2>
                <p class="about-section-lede">
                To call a study incomplete is not simply to say that it is unfinished. Incompletion can belong to different parts of a research project, in different ways, and for different reasons.
                </p>
            </div>

            <div class="about-prose">
                <p>
                Some research is unfinished because it is still developing — an argument is being tested, evidence is still being gathered, a method is being revised. The next stage is already in sight, even if it has not yet arrived.
                </p>
                <p>
                Other research becomes incomplete in a different way. A project is interrupted. A dataset remains partial. An experiment fails. Funding ends. Access disappears. A researcher changes direction. A project is abandoned, perhaps permanently.
                </p>
                <p>
                And sometimes the incompletion belongs to the question itself. The evidence may never be sufficient. The object of study may resist a definitive account. Several explanations may remain possible. The research may clarify a problem without resolving it.
                </p>
            </div>

            <div class="about-incompletion-types">

                <div class="inc-type">
                <span class="inc-label">Conceptual</span>
                <span class="inc-desc">An argument has not settled, or competing interpretations remain in play.</span>
                </div>

                <div class="inc-type">
                <span class="inc-label">Empirical</span>
                <span class="inc-desc">The evidence is incomplete, contradictory, inaccessible, or insufficient to support a definitive claim.</span>
                </div>

                <div class="inc-type">
                <span class="inc-label">Methodological</span>
                <span class="inc-desc">A method has failed, produced unexpected results, or revealed a problem that changes the direction of the research.</span>
                </div>

                <div class="inc-type">
                <span class="inc-label">Historical</span>
                <span class="inc-desc">Part of the record has been lost, a project was interrupted, or the conditions of production are themselves part of what needs to be understood.</span>
                </div>

                <div class="inc-type">
                <span class="inc-label">Formal</span>
                <span class="inc-desc">The work may not yet have a stable shape, or may take a fragmentary, serial, or otherwise unconventional form.</span>
                </div>

                <div class="inc-type">
                <span class="inc-label">Temporal</span>
                <span class="inc-desc">A study may be complete enough to publish at one moment while later developments alter its argument, interpretation, or significance.</span>
                </div>

            </div>

            <div class="about-incompletion-footer">
                <p class="small">
                These forms can overlap. A methodological failure can produce an unresolved argument. Missing evidence can change the question. An interruption can become part of the history of a project.
                </p>
                
                <a class="practice-link" href="{{ site.baseurl }}/manifesto/">Read the full argument on incompletion →</a>
            </div>
        </section>

        <!-- What the rule does -->
        <section class="about-section" id="practice">
            <div class="about-section-head">
                <div class="kicker">What the rule does</div>
                <h2>Four practices<br>that follow from it.</h2>
            </div>

            <div class="practice-list">

                <div class="practice">
                <div class="practice-num">01</div>
                <div class="practice-body">
                    <h3>Projects, not papers.</h3>
                    <p class="small">
                    Research enters the journal as a persistent project — not an article. A project has a beginning, a sequence of installments, and a state. It may change direction, contradict itself, or stop. All of these things are the record.
                    </p>
                    <a class="practice-link" onclick="show('projects')">See how a project works →</a>
                </div>
                </div>

                <div class="practice">
                <div class="practice-num">02</div>
                <div class="practice-body">
                    <h3>Installments, not issues.</h3>
                    <p class="small">
                    Research unfolds through time. A project publishes installments as they are written, each dated, each kept. No installment supersedes another; the second is not replaced by the third. The reader sees the sequence, not its latest state.
                    </p>
                    <a class="practice-link" onclick="show('reading')">Read an installment →</a>
                </div>
                </div>

                <div class="practice">
                <div class="practice-num">03</div>
                <div class="practice-body">
                    <h3>Everything is in the archive.</h3>
                    <p class="small">
                    Earlier work is not archived out of view. It remains part of the current record — searchable, linkable, cited. The archive is not a separate place for old things. It is the same record, seen from a longer view.
                    </p>
                    <div class="sidebar-coming-badge">
                        <span class="pulse-dot"></span>
                        <span>Coming Soon:</span>
                    </div>
                    <a class="practice-link" onclick="show('archive')">Enter the archive →</a>
                </div>
                </div>

                <div class="practice">
                <div class="practice-num">04</div>
                <div class="practice-body">
                    <h3>Absence is indexed.</h3>
                    <p class="small">
                    Missing installments, abandoned directions, and unanswered questions are recorded alongside finished ones. The journal keeps a parallel index — the Missing Index — for what was expected, but did not arrive. Absence is a record, not an error.
                    </p>
                    <div class="sidebar-coming-badge">
                        <span class="pulse-dot"></span>
                        <span>Coming Soon:</span>
                    </div>
                    <a class="practice-link" onclick="show('missing')">Open the Missing Index →</a>
                    
                </div>
                </div>

            </div>

        <!-- What the rule costs -->
        <section class="about-section" id="cost">
            <div class="about-section-head">
                <div class="kicker">What the rule costs</div>
                <h2>What this journal<br>cannot be.</h2>
                <p class="about-section-lede">
                A rule is defined not only by what it makes possible, but by what it refuses. These are the things the journal cannot do, by design.
                </p>
            </div>

            <div class="cost-list">
                <div class="cost">
                    <div class="cost-num">—</div>
                        <div class="cost-body">
                            <h3>It cannot be cited as a single source.</h3>
                            <p class="small">
                            A project's latest installment is not its final position. To cite the journal is to cite one installment, at one date, in one state — never the project as a whole. This is inconvenient for citations. It is honest about what projects are.
                            </p>
                        </div>
                    </div>
                

                <div class="cost">
                <div class="cost-num">—</div>
                <div class="cost-body">
                    <h3>It cannot be read linearly.</h3>
                    <p class="small">
                    The journal has no beginning, middle, and end. There is no issue one should start with. There is no recommended reading order. The reader is expected to find their own path through the record, as they would through an archive.
                    </p>
                </div>
                </div>

                <div class="cost">
                <div class="cost-num">—</div>
                <div class="cost-body">
                    <h3>It cannot be finished.</h3>
                    <p class="small">
                    There is no state in which the journal is complete. Every project either continues, or stops — and stopping is not completion. The record grows, but it never arrives. This is the cost of refusing to disappear things.
                    </p>
                </div>
                </div>

                <div class="cost">
                <div class="cost-num">—</div>
                <div class="cost-body">
                    <h3>It cannot promise answers.</h3>
                    <p class="small">
                    Most of what the journal publishes is unfinished, unresolved, or withdrawn. The reader who comes looking for conclusions will find few. What they will find instead is a clear account of why there aren't more.
                    </p>
                </div>
                </div>

            </div>
        </section>

        <!-- FAQ -->
        <section class="about-section" id="faq">
        <div class="about-section-head">
            <div class="kicker">FAQ</div>
            <h2>Common questions,<br>answered directly.</h2>
        </div>

        <div class="about-faq">

            <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
                <span>Is unfinished work still scholarship?</span>
                <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
                <p>Only if you assume scholarship begins at publication. This journal rejects that assumption.</p>
                <p>Research begins long before a manuscript acquires the appearance of completion. It develops through uncertainty, hesitation, revision, and return. Conventional publishing conceals those moments. Here, they are preserved.</p>
            </div>
            </details>

            <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
                <span>Are installments drafts?</span>
                <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
                <p><strong>No.</strong> A draft exists <em>prior</em> to publication. An installment is a publication — the most complete articulation of a project at a given moment. Later installments may revise or contradict earlier ones, but that never diminishes what has already been published.</p>
            </div>
            </details>

            <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
                <span>Is this peer reviewed?</span>
                <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
                <p>Not in the conventional, blind sense. Each installment passes through editorial review before publication — a sustained dialogue with the editorial team, not an anonymous verdict rendered once. Rigour is demonstrated across a season, not certified in a single moment.</p>
            </div>
            </details>

            <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
                <span>What is an "incomplete study"?</span>
                <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
                <p>Not a fragment. An incomplete study is research that acknowledges its own provisionality — a genuine contribution that stays open to transformation. Its incompleteness is <em>epistemic</em>: it reflects the fact that every account of a complex phenomenon remains partial.</p>
            </div>
            </details>

            <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
                <span>Why preserve revisions?</span>
                <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
                <p>Because revision is evidence of thought. Most publishing systems overwrite earlier versions; this journal does not. An earlier position is not an embarrassment to erase — it is part of the history of an inquiry. Knowledge has a history. Here it is preserved.</p>
            </div>
            </details>

            <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
                <span>Can a project remain unfinished forever?</span>
                <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">+</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
                <p>Perhaps. Not every inquiry naturally concludes — some questions generate further questions. Completion is not the measure of success. A project succeeds if it advances understanding, even when that advance is revealing how complex a problem really is, rather than resolving it.</p>
            </div>
            </details>

        </div>
