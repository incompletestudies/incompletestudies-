    <section id="manifesto" class="about-section">

      <div class="section-header">
        <div class="section-heading-group">
          <span class="section-number">01</span>
          <span class="section-title">THE JOURNAL</span>
        </div>
        <span class="section-meta">Why incomplete research matters</span>
      </div>

      <div class="content">

        <p><em>The Journal of Incomplete Studies</em> is an ongoing online publication for research that remains <em>incomplete</em>. It publishes work in progress, provisional arguments, uncertain findings, interrupted projects, failed experiments, missing data, fragments, abandoned research, inconclusive results, and unresolved questions.</p>

        <p>But the journal is interested in something more than collecting unfinished work. Its title operates in <strong>two senses</strong>.</p>

        <!-- DUAL MEANING CARD -->
        <div class="dual-meaning-card">

          <div class="dual-meaning-col">
            <span class="dual-label">Sense 1</span>
            <span class="dual-title">A Journal Containing</span>
            <span class="dual-title dual-title-sub">Incomplete Studies</span>
          </div>

          <div class="dual-divider">
            <span class="dual-divider-line"></span>
            <span class="dual-divider-label">also read as</span>
            <span class="dual-divider-line"></span>
          </div>

          <div class="dual-meaning-col">
            <span class="dual-label">Sense 2</span>
            <span class="dual-title">A Journal Studying</span>
            <span class="dual-title dual-title-sub">Incompleteness Itself</span>
          </div>

        </div>

        <p>Research is usually made public at a particular point of arrival. By then, much of what happened along the way has often been removed from view. Uncertainty has been resolved or contained. Detours have disappeared. Failed attempts have become invisible. Contradictions have been smoothed away. Questions that once shaped the work may survive only as footnotes, if they survive at all.</p>

        <p>What remains is the research in the form it was able to take when it was published. <em>The Journal of Incomplete Studies</em> attends to <span class="highlight"><strong>what remains outside that frame</strong></span>.</p>

        <p>This does not mean treating every unfinished piece of research as valuable simply because it is unfinished, nor does it mean imagining that completion is something research should resist. It means asking what becomes possible when research can be encountered before its uncertainties have been resolved, while its directions are still changing, or after a project has stopped without producing the conclusion it expected to reach.</p>

        <p>A failed experiment may contain something that its successful replacement no longer shows. A discarded argument may tell us something about the argument that followed. A missing piece may alter our understanding of the whole. A question may remain a question. <strong>These are not necessarily stages in a sequence, nor conditions to be overcome.</strong> Research may move between them, remain within one, return to another, or never arrive anywhere else.</p>

        <p><strong>In progress. Provisional. Uncertain. Interrupted. Abandoned. Inconclusive. Unresolved.</strong></p>

        <p>The journal begins from the possibility that these states are not merely obstacles on the way to knowledge, but can themselves become part of what there is to know. <strong>The research does not begin with the published version, nor does it necessarily end there.</strong></p>

        <div style="margin: 1.5rem 0;">
          <a href="/manifesto/" class="btn btn-primary">Read the full manifesto →</a>
        </div>

      </div>
    </section>

    <section id="what-we-mean" class="about-section">

      <div class="section-header">
        <div class="section-heading-group">
          <span class="section-number">02</span>
          <span class="section-title">INCOMPLETION</span>
        </div>
        <span class="section-meta">The different conditions of unfinished research</span>
      </div>

      <div class="content">

        <p>To call a study <em>incomplete</em> is not simply to say that it is unfinished. Some research is unfinished because it is still developing. An argument is being tested, evidence is still being gathered, a method is being revised. The next stage is already in sight, even if it has not yet arrived.</p>

        <p>But other research becomes incomplete in a different way. A project is interrupted. A dataset remains partial. An experiment fails. A method produces results that cannot be reconciled with the original question. Funding ends. Access disappears. A researcher changes direction. An argument reaches an impasse. A project is abandoned, perhaps permanently.</p>

        <p>And sometimes the incompletion belongs to the question itself. The evidence may never be sufficient. The object of study may resist a definitive account. Several explanations may remain possible. The research may clarify a problem without resolving it. These are different conditions, even when they produce something that looks similar from the outside: <strong>a study without a final conclusion.</strong></p>

        <p><span class="highlight"><strong>Incomplete does not describe a single stage of research.</strong></span> Nor does it necessarily describe something waiting to become complete. It can describe a moment, a condition, a limitation, a failure, an interruption, or an unresolved problem within the work.</p>

        <p>Incompletion can belong to different parts of a study:</p>

        <div class="incomplete-types">

          <div class="type-item">
            <span class="type-label">Conceptual</span>
            <span class="type-desc">An argument has not settled, or competing interpretations remain in play.</span>
          </div>

          <div class="type-item">
            <span class="type-label">Empirical</span>
            <span class="type-desc">The evidence is incomplete, contradictory, inaccessible, or insufficient to support a definitive claim.</span>
          </div>

          <div class="type-item">
            <span class="type-label">Methodological</span>
            <span class="type-desc">A method has failed, produced unexpected results, or revealed a problem that changes the direction of the research.</span>
          </div>

          <div class="type-item">
            <span class="type-label">Historical</span>
            <span class="type-desc">Part of the record has been lost, a project was interrupted, or the conditions under which the research was produced are themselves part of what needs to be understood.</span>
          </div>

          <div class="type-item">
            <span class="type-label">Formal</span>
            <span class="type-desc">The work may not yet have a stable shape, or may take a fragmentary, serial, provisional, or otherwise unconventional form.</span>
          </div>

          <div class="type-item">
            <span class="type-label">Temporal</span>
            <span class="type-desc">A study may be complete enough to publish at one moment while later developments alter its argument, interpretation, or significance.</span>
          </div>

        </div>

        <p>These forms can overlap. A methodological failure can produce an unresolved argument. Missing evidence can change the question. An interruption can become part of the history of a project. A change in direction can make an earlier version newly significant.</p>

        <p>This is one reason <em>incompletion</em> is difficult to recognise from the finished publication alone. Research is usually made public at a particular point of arrival. By then, a draft has become an article, preliminary findings have become results, and questions have been shaped into conclusions. The approach that failed may have disappeared behind the one that worked. The discarded argument may survive only as a trace. The uncertainty that once shaped the research may have been resolved, contained, or simply removed.</p>

        <p>The finished publication can therefore give the impression that the research was always moving towards the form in which we encounter it. <em>But research rarely happens that way.</em> It proceeds through uncertainty, repetition, hesitation, wrong turns, partial answers, changes of method, unexpected findings, and decisions about what not to pursue. Some of these become part of the published account. Others are compressed into a methodology section or reduced to a footnote. <strong>Many leave no trace at all.</strong></p>

        <p><em>The Journal of Incomplete Studies</em> begins with an interest in that difference: between <em>the research as it happened</em> and the research that can finally be presented. That does not mean preserving every discarded thought or treating every unfinished project as significant. It means asking what may be lost when research is only encountered after these conditions have been resolved, edited out, or left behind.</p>

        <p>A failed experiment may tell us something that its successful replacement no longer shows. A partial dataset may expose the limits of a claim more clearly than a complete one. An abandoned direction may explain why the research took another path. A contradiction may reveal a problem that a conclusion would conceal. A question may remain important even when the project that produced it has stopped.</p>

        <p>Incompletion, in this sense, is not a single category of work. It is a way of encountering research in relation to <em>what it could not settle, what it did not reach, what interrupted it, or what it left behind.</em> Some incompletion can be resolved. Some cannot. Some belongs to a particular moment in the life of a project; some becomes part of its history. Some leads somewhere else. Some marks a boundary. <strong>Some simply remains.</strong></p>

        <p><span class="highlight"><strong>The journal is interested in what those differences can tell us about research itself.</strong></span></p>

      </div>
    </section>

    <section id="for-readers" class="about-section">

      <div class="section-header">
        <div class="section-heading-group">
          <span class="section-number">03</span>
          <span class="section-title">For Readers</span>
        </div>
        <span class="section-meta">How to follow a line of thought</span>
      </div>

      <div class="content">

        <p><em>The Journal of Incomplete Studies</em> is not designed around a single way of reading. There is no required starting point, and no need to encounter a study only once it has settled into its final form.</p>

        <p>Begin anywhere. Browse the <a href="/archive/">current season</a>, enter through a project that catches your attention, or arrive through a question that leads somewhere unexpected. An unfinished argument can be read on its own, or alongside another study, another discipline, another way of asking the same thing.</p>

        <p>What matters here is not only where a study arrives, but what remains visible along the way: uncertainty, contradiction, missing evidence, failed experiments, abandoned directions, revisions, and questions that have not yet become answers.</p>

        <p>Return when something changes. New installments may alter an argument, complicate a finding, reopen a question, or take the work somewhere entirely different. Earlier versions remain part of the archive, so the history of the research stays visible rather than being replaced by its latest form.</p>

        <p>And stay with what does not resolve. A fragment may connect to another project. A contradiction may open a different question. A failed experiment may become useful elsewhere. Research can continue to matter beyond the point where a project itself stops. <strong>What happens next is not always determined by the project itself. Sometimes it begins with another reader.</strong></p>

        <p>Read across. Come back. Notice what changes. Follow the work beyond the point where it was first published.</p>

    <section id="for-contributors" class="about-section">

      <div class="section-header">
        <div class="section-heading-group">
          <span class="section-number">04</span>
          <span class="section-title">For Contributors</span>
        </div>
        <span class="section-meta">How to bring research into the journal</span>
      </div>

      <div class="content">

        <p>If you are working with research that has not reached the place you expected it to reach, you may already recognise something of your project here. Perhaps the argument is still changing. Perhaps the evidence has complicated the question rather than answered it. Perhaps a method has led somewhere unexpected. Perhaps the project was interrupted, abandoned, or simply stopped before you knew what it might become.</p>

        <p>Perhaps the research is not ready to become a finished article. That does not necessarily mean it is not ready to be read. A contribution to <em>The Journal of Incomplete Studies</em> begins from the research as it stands, rather than from the requirement that it first arrive somewhere else.</p>

        <div class="float-wrapper">

          <div class="float-box">
            <span class="float-box-label">A project can begin here:</span>
            <ul class="details-list">
              <li>An argument that has not settled</li>
              <li>Evidence that has changed the question</li>
              <li>A method that led somewhere unexpected</li>
              <li>A project interrupted along the way</li>
              <li>A direction left behind</li>
              <li>A question that remained after the project stopped</li>
            </ul>
          </div>

          <div class="float-text">
            <p>These are not requirements or categories to fit neatly into. They are possible points of entry. What matters is the relationship between the research and the point at which you choose to make it public.</p>

            <p>Writing for <em>The Journal of Incomplete Studies</em> means writing in public while the research is still changing. The question may remain active. The evidence may be partial. An argument may have more than one possible direction. A method may have failed without making clear what should replace it. Publication does not have to wait until all of this has been resolved. Here, publication can mark a particular moment in the life of an inquiry.</p>
          </div>

        </div>

        <p>An installment is neither a final account nor simply a draft made public before it is ready. It is a considered scholarly act: an account of where the research has reached, what it can currently claim, and what remains uncertain. <span class="highlight">Writing in public means taking responsibility for the research as it is, without pretending that this is where it ends.</span></p>

        <p>That responsibility matters. Incompletion is not a reason to lower the standards of the work. An installment should be precise about its claims, attentive to its evidence, and clear about what has and has not been established. Uncertainty can remain part of the research without becoming a substitute for argument. A failed method can remain a failure. A contradiction does not need to be smoothed away simply because it is difficult to explain. <strong>The work can be rigorous without being made to appear settled.</strong></p>

        <div class="float-wrapper">

          <div class="float-box">
            <span class="float-box-label">Across a season:</span>
            <ul class="details-list">
              <li>An initial installment</li>
              <li>Monthly publication across 3–4 months</li>
              <li>Editorial dialogue before each installment</li>
              <li>Conversation with the contributor cohort</li>
              <li>No submission or publication fees</li>
            </ul>
          </div>

          <div class="float-text">
            <p>A project enters the journal through an initial installment and continues across the season. The research can change between appearances. New evidence may alter an argument. A question may shift. A method may be reconsidered. An unexpected result may redirect the work. The next installment does not have to confirm the last.</p>

            <p>This is why the journal is structured across time. The installments are not chapters waiting to be assembled into a finished paper. Each marks a different point in the movement of the research. The commitment is therefore not simply to producing a sequence of texts. It is to staying with the inquiry as it changes, including when the direction becomes less certain, more complicated, or difficult to sustain. <span class="highlight">The commitment is not to producing pages, but to remaining faithful to a question across time.</span></p>
          </div>
        </div>

        <p>Earlier installments remain in the archive. Later versions do not replace them. A contributor may revise an argument, contradict an earlier position, extend the project, change direction, or stop altogether. What follows can change the meaning of what came before, but it does not erase the point from which the research proceeded. <strong>Research can change without rewriting its own history.</strong></p>

        <p>A contribution therefore becomes part of a record: what was thought, what was attempted, what changed, and what remained unresolved. Another researcher may encounter that record differently. A question may lead into another project. A failed approach may become useful elsewhere. An abandoned direction may acquire significance later. Or the project may simply stop.</p>

        <p>Bring the argument before it settles. Bring the result before it becomes certain. Bring the question if the answer never arrived.</p>

    <section id="how-it-works" class="about-section">

      <div class="section-header">
        <div class="section-heading-group">
          <span class="section-number">05</span>
          <span class="section-title">The Form</span>
        </div>
        <span class="section-meta">How research unfolds here</span>
      </div>

      <div class="content">

        <p><em>The Journal of Incomplete Studies</em> is organised around a simple structure: research enters the journal as a project, appears through a series of installments, and unfolds across a season alongside the work of other researchers. The form gives the research a rhythm without requiring it to arrive at a predetermined destination.</p>

        <h4 class="subhead">Four terms worth knowing</h4>

        <div class="term-glossary">

          <div class="term-item">
            <div class="term-header">
              <span class="term-number">01</span>
              <span class="term-name">PROJECT</span>
            </div>
            <span class="term-desc">The research itself as it moves through the journal — an investigation, a question, a body of work that develops over time. A project may change direction, encounter problems, pause, resume, or stop. It is not a finished work divided into installments, but research taking shape through them.</span>
          </div>

          <div class="term-item">
            <div class="term-header">
              <span class="term-number">02</span>
              <span class="term-name">SEASON</span>
            </div>
            <span class="term-desc">A shared period of publication in which a cohort of researchers publishes alongside one another. It establishes the rhythm and duration of the journal's publication cycle, but does not determine how long a project itself will continue.</span>
          </div>

          <div class="term-item">
            <div class="term-header">
              <span class="term-number">03</span>
              <span class="term-name">INSTALLMENT</span>
            </div>
            <span class="term-desc">The journal's basic unit. Neither a conventional article nor simply a chapter in a larger work — research made public at a particular moment, carrying the traces of what came before and the possibility of what might follow.</span>
          </div>

          <div class="term-item">
            <div class="term-header">
              <span class="term-number">04</span>
              <span class="term-name">COHORT</span>
            </div>
            <span class="term-desc">The group of researchers whose projects are published within the same season. Their projects remain independent, but the shared period creates opportunities for exchange, conversation, and unexpected proximity across disciplines, methods, questions, and forms.</span>
          </div>

        </div>

        <p>The form begins with a <strong>proposal</strong>. Once a cohort is selected, each project is published once a month across the season. Between installments, contributors remain in editorial dialogue with the journal, giving the research time to respond to new evidence, changing questions, problems encountered along the way, or simply the passage of time.</p>

        <p>An installment is not simply a portion of a larger article. It can take different forms according to what the research requires: an argument, a result, a methodological account, a fragment, a problem, or a change in direction. Each installment should stand as a considered piece of research while remaining part of the larger movement of the project. <strong>The next installment does not have to confirm the last.</strong></p>

        <p>The cohort introduces another dimension to the form. Researchers are working on independent projects, but they are doing so within a shared period of publication. They encounter one another's work without needing to produce a common theme or collective argument. A project in one discipline may sit beside a very different method, question, or form. The proximity itself can create connections that would not otherwise have been there.</p>

        <p>The season is therefore a commitment to a period of publication, not a prescribed lifespan for the research. A project commits to the rhythm of the season, but it does not have to end when the season ends. It may continue elsewhere, change form, lead into another project, reach a conclusion, or simply stop.</p>

        <p>A season ends. The publication cycle ends. <strong>What happens to the research after that is its own question.</strong></p>

      <section id="faq" class="about-section">

        <div class="section-header">
          <div class="section-heading-group">
            <span class="section-number">06</span>
            <span class="section-title">FAQ</span>
          </div>
          <span class="section-meta">Philosophy &amp; practice</span>
        </div>

        <div class="faq-accordion">
          <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
              <span>Is unfinished work still scholarship?</span>
              <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">▸</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
              <p>Only if you assume scholarship begins at publication. We reject that assumption.</p>
              <p>Research begins long before a manuscript acquires the appearance of completion. It develops through uncertainty, hesitation, revision, and return. Conventional publishing conceals those moments. We preserve the argument while it is still becoming.</p>
            </div>
          </details>

          <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
              <span>Are installments drafts?</span>
              <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">▸</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
              <p><strong>No.</strong> A draft exists <em>prior</em> to publication. An installment is a <em>publication</em> — the most complete articulation of a project at a given moment. Later installments may revise or contradict earlier ones, but that never diminishes what has already been published.</p>
            </div>
          </details>

          <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
              <span>Is this peer reviewed?</span>
              <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">▸</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
              <p>Not in the conventional, blind sense. Each installment passes through editorial review before publication — a sustained dialogue with the editorial team, not an anonymous verdict rendered once. Rigour is demonstrated across a season, not certified in a single moment.</p>
            </div>
          </details>

          <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
              <span>What is an "incomplete study"?</span>
              <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">▸</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
              <p>It is not a fragment. An incomplete study is research that acknowledges its own provisionality — a genuine contribution that stays open to transformation. Its incompleteness is <em>epistemic</em>: it reflects the fact that every account of a complex phenomenon remains partial.</p>
            </div>
          </details>

          <details class="faq-item">
            <summary class="faq-question">
              <span>Why preserve revisions?</span>
              <span class="faq-icon" aria-hidden="true">▸</span>
            </summary>
            <div class="faq-answer">
