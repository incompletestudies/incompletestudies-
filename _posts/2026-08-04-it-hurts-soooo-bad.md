Overview:

What does it mean to approach the archive as infrastructure? This installment argues that digital archives are not simply repositories of content, but active infrastructures that shape how knowledge is produced, accessed, and contested. Drawing on media theory, infrastructure studies, and archival practice, it outlines a framework for understanding digital archives as material, political, and temporal systems.

Content:

The Archive as Infrastructure

The term "infrastructure" is often used to describe systems that are invisible, taken for granted, and foundational to other activities. In this sense, digital archives are infrastructures: they enable research, preserve cultural memory, and structure access to information. But they are also contested sites of power, politics, and forgetting.

Following *Star and Ruhleder* (1996), infrastructure is not a fixed object but a relational concept that becomes visible when it breaks down. Digital archives break down in predictable ways—through format obsolescence, platform shifts, and institutional neglect—but also in ways that reveal the assumptions embedded in their design.

Three Dimensions of Archival Infrastructure

- Material – The hardware, software, and protocols that support digital preservation.

  - Political – The governance structures, funding models, and legal frameworks that shape access.

- Temporal – The rhythms and durations of preservation, maintenance, and decay.

Each dimension intersects with the others, creating complex conditions for the survival and interpretation of digital cultural heritage.

Implications for the Digital Archives Initiative

The Digital Archives Initiative (DAI) is designed as a testbed for these ideas. It operates across institutional and community contexts, experimenting with different preservation models and access protocols. By treating the archive as infrastructure, the DAI aims to make visible the conditions of digital memory and to develop alternative approaches that are more open, equitable, and resilient.
